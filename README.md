# Yb Molecular Complex Toolkit

A single-file, browser-only tool with two calculators for Yb(III) molecular complexes:

1. Crystal-field splitting (SOCF): crystal-field splitting of the Yb(III) ²F₇/₂ and ²F₅/₂ manifolds from a crystal structure (CIF) and an absorption spectrum.
2. Oscillator strength: absorption cross section, oscillator strength f₁₂ and FWHM from an absorption spectrum or from peak parameters.

Everything runs locally in your browser. Uploaded files are never sent to a server.

Live page: https://yb-molc.github.io/Yb-molecular-complex/


## Tab 1: Crystal-field splitting (SOCF)

### What it computes

The method follows the spin-orbit-coupled crystal-field (SOCF) approach of Li et al., Chem. Sci. 2024, 15, 12451 (and its SI). For Yb(III) (4f¹³) it builds the Hamiltonian

H = −ξ · l·s + H_CF

in the 14-function |m_l, m_s⟩ basis (ξ = 2870 cm⁻¹ by default), diagonalises it, and reports the 7 Kramers doublets:

- (1,2), (3,4), (5,6), (7,8): ground manifold ²F₇/₂
- (9,10), (11,12), (13,14): excited manifold ²F₅/₂

### How to use it, step by step

1. Upload the CIF of your complex. The tool finds the Yb atom and every donor atom (Cl, O, N, S, …) inside the cutoff radius (default 3.0 Å), including atoms generated by symmetry operations. If several Yb atoms exist, choose one in the *Yb atom* menu. Check the donor table: for YbCl₃(2-MeTHF)₃ you expect 3 Cl and 3 O.
2. Upload the absorption CSV (two columns: x, absorbance) and choose the x unit (nm, eV, cm⁻¹ or THz).
3. (Optional) Upload an emission CSV. The two strongest peaks are used to propose the ground-state splitting (their energy difference). Treat this as a candidate: a vibrational shoulder looks the same at room temperature.
4. Peak search: set the energy window (default 1.20–1.50 eV), the minimum prominence and the number of peaks to skip. Press 'Find peaks again' if you changed them. The found peaks fill the three target boxes.
5. Check the three target energies E(9,10), E(11,12), E(13,14), measured from E(1,2).
   - Clear a box if that line is not observed or is a hot band (typical for E(11,12)). At least two energies are required.
6. Ground splitting: enter the experimental E(3,4) − E(1,2) in meV and tick. Include ground splitting as a fit target if you want it used in the fit.
7. Leave Also fit the charge split off unless you have at least four targets.
8. Press Run calculation.

### What happens inside (the calculation)

1. Geometry. The CIF cell is converted to Cartesian coordinates, symmetry operations generate neighbouring atoms, and each donor is expressed in spherical coordinates (R, θ, φ) with Yb at the origin.
2. Point charges. Each donor gets a charge proportional to its Pauling electronegativity, normalised so that the charges sum to −3 (O −0.526, Cl −0.474 for a 3 O + 3 Cl sphere).
3. Crystal-field coefficients. A_q^k = K · Σᵢ (−1)^q Qᵢ C_{−q}^k(θᵢ, φᵢ) / Rᵢ^(k+1), with K = 116140.6 cm⁻¹·Å and k = 1…6.
4. Radial integrals. B_q^k = A_q^k · ⟨r^k⟩. The values of ⟨r^k⟩ are not given in the SI, so they are treated as ⟨r^k⟩ = a·ρ^k with two fit parameters (a, ρ).
5. Hamiltonian. The spin-orbit term and the crystal-field term (from Racah tensor matrix elements, evaluated by Gauss–Legendre quadrature) are assembled in the 14×14 basis. Free-ion check: −1.5ξ (×8) and +2ξ (×6).
6. Diagonalisation. The 14×14 Hermitian matrix is embedded in a real 28×28 matrix and solved by Jacobi rotations, giving 7 doublets.
7. Quantities compared with experiment** (definitions from SI Table S13):
   - Ground splitting = E(3,4) − E(1,2)
   - Excited splitting = E(11,12) − E(9,10)
   - Transition energies = E(9,10), E(11,12), E(13,14) − E(1,2)
   - Excited-band width = E(13,14) − E(9,10), used when E(11,12) is not observed
8. Fit. a and ρ are adjusted (Nelder–Mead, 9 starting points) to minimize the squared residuals between calculated and observed energies.
9. Output. Energy-level diagram, absorption spectrum with calculated lines, results table, residuals, charges used, and warnings.

### Reading the result

- A residual below a few meV is good. Residuals above 10 meV are flagged.
- With three or four targets and two fit parameters there is almost no redundancy. Do not over-interpret a small residual.
- a and ρ are strongly correlated, so their individual values have no physical meaning.

### Limitations

- Point-charge model: no covalency, no vibronic coupling, no charge transfer.
- Room-temperature spectra have broad lines mixed with hot bands. Low-temperature absorption and emission are needed to assign E(11,12) and to confirm the ground splitting.
- The radial parameterisation (a·ρ^k) and the fitting procedure are this tool's own choices, not taken from the paper. The rotation scan of the charges in the paper is not needed here because the energies are invariant to a rigid rotation.

## Tab 2: Oscillator strength

### What it computes

For a single, roughly isolated absorption line it returns the absorption coefficient, the integrated absorption cross section, the oscillator strength f₁₂ and the FWHM. Lorentzian and Gaussian line shapes are both fitted and shown side by side.

### How to use it, step by step

1. Sample (left panel): choose the solvent (or type its refractive index n_r), the concentration in mM, the path length in cm, and the peak wavelength in nm. The fit window is centred on this wavelength.
2. From spectrum:
   1. Upload the CSV (two columns: x, y) and choose the x unit.
   2. Choose what the y column contains: log₁₀ absorbance A, or natural absorbance −ln(T). Choosing the wrong one changes f₁₂ by a factor of ln 10 ≈ 2.3.
   3. Set the fit window half-width (default ±6 nm) and the baseline (linear, constant, or none; default linear).
   4. Press Calculate.
3. From peak parameters: enter the peak height, its type (log₁₀ or natural), and the FWHM in GHz, THz, nm, cm⁻¹ or meV. Both line shapes are evaluated with the same height and FWHM.
4. Use Download results as CSV to save the table.

### The calculation

1. Absorption coefficient: α = −ln(I_out/I_in)/L. A log₁₀ absorbance is multiplied by ln 10 first.
2. Frequency axis: ν = c/λ. The spectrum inside the window is fitted with α(ν) = A·g(ν; ν₀, w) + baseline. The Lorentzian is g = (w/2)² / ((ν − ν₀)² + (w/2)²); the Gaussian is g = exp(−4 ln 2 · (ν − ν₀)² / w²), with w the FWHM.
3. Integral over angular frequency (ω = 2πν):
   - Lorentzian: ∫α dω = 2π · A · (π/2) · w = 2π² · A · γ, with γ = w/2.
   - Gaussian: ∫α dω = 2π · A · w · √(π / (4 ln 2)).
4. Cross section: ∫σ dω = ∫α dω / N, with N = c_mM · N_A (1 mM = 1 mol/m³; 4 mM → 2.4089 × 10²⁴ m⁻³).
5. Oscillator strength: f₁₂ = [2 ε₀ m c n_r / (π e²)] · ∫σ dω. For n_r = 1.406 the prefactor is 8.4315 × 10⁴ m⁻² s.
6. Extra outputs: peak α, peak αL, peak ε (M⁻¹ cm⁻¹), peak σ (cm²), FWHM in GHz, nm, cm⁻¹ and meV, fit R², the percentage of the fitted line area inside the window, and a model-independent trapezoid integral over the window.

### Checks that were done

- With ∫α dω = 1.1018 × 10¹⁴ m⁻¹ s⁻¹, 4 mM and n_r = 1.406 the tool gives f₁₂ = 3.8565 × 10⁻⁶.

### Settings that change the answer

| Setting | Effect |
|---------|--------|
| Peak wavelength | Shifts the fit window. 980 nm and 981 nm give different results with a ±6 nm window. |
| Window | A narrow window truncates the Lorentzian tails and constrains the baseline. |
| Baseline | None / constant / linear can change f₁₂ by several percent or more. |
| y type | log₁₀ vs natural gives a factor of 2.303. |
| Concentration and n_r | f₁₂ is proportional to n_r / concentration. |

Report the window and baseline together with any f₁₂ value.
